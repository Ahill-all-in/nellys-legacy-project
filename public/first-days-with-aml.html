Danell's Journey › The first days

Nobody sits you down first.

Before Danell was diagnosed, the only reason she had ever been in a hospital was to have a baby. We had no idea how any of it worked. Then one blood test turned into a transfusion inside twenty minutes, an ambulance to another city, and a five-week admission that started the same night — and at no point was there a meeting where somebody explained the plan first.

That is not bad care. It is what acute leukemia requires. But nobody warns you, so this page is the warning. Here is what happened to us, the words you are about to be buried in, and the one rule worth memorizing tonight.

Written by Austin, Danell's husband. I am not a doctor and nothing here is medical advice — every number below is sourced, but your team's instructions always win.

January 2025

How fast it actually moved

People imagine a diagnosis, then a week of tests, then a treatment plan. That is how a lot of cancers go. It is not how this one goes.

  • The blood draw

    Danell had been unwell for weeks. A routine blood test at our community hospital in Georgetown came back with a hemoglobin of 2.4 — normal for a woman is around 12 — and a white blood cell count near 140,000, where normal is under 11,000.

    Three quarters of what was in that sample were blasts. Seventy-five percent. Her marrow had been so completely taken over that mature B cells were less than half of one percent of what the lab could find.

  • Twenty minutes later, the first transfusion

    Not after a consult. Not the next morning. They started putting blood back into her almost immediately, because at those numbers there is nothing to discuss.

  • Transferred to UK

    A community hospital is not equipped for acute leukemia. She was moved to the University of Kentucky, where the Markey Cancer Center is.

  • Straight into a room — not a hallway

    They did not park her on a gurney in the corridor like every ER show you have ever seen. There is a real reason for that, and it is further down this page.

  • Up to the 11th floor, and chemo the same admission

    The blood and marrow transplant unit. She started “7+3” induction chemotherapy, and that first stay lasted five weeks.

Why nobody was waiting

A white count over 100,000 has a name — hyperleukocytosis — and the American Society of Hematology puts it plainly: “Because of excessive early mortality, HL in AML is a medical emergency, and treatment should start immediately.” It shows up in roughly 5 to 20 percent of new AML cases.

The danger is leukostasis. Myeloid blast cells are big and stiff compared to normal blood cells, so when there are enough of them they physically clog the smallest vessels — mostly in the lungs and brain — and the blood stops getting through. Starting treatment fast is what prevents that. So if it feels like the hospital skipped the part where they explain things to you, this is why.

The Vocabulary

Five numbers, in the order you'll meet them

Within about a day you will be expected to follow conversations built entirely out of these, and then repeat them accurately to worried relatives. Nobody hands you the glossary. Here it is, with Danell's actual numbers from diagnosis day so you can see what “bad” looks like.

Reference ranges vary slightly by lab — yours may print different numbers on the page. Also worth knowing: since 2022 certain genetic findings allow an AML diagnosis below the classic 20% blast threshold, and the two major classification systems set that bar differently. If your blast count is lower than 20 and they still said AML, that is why.

Acute versus chronic — the difference that explains the urgency

Everyone assumes it means fast versus slow. It's more specific than that. In chronic leukemia the cells do grow up, so they still do some of their job; there are just far too many. In acute leukemia the cells never mature at all. As one specialist puts it, those stumped baby cells have zero function.

So an acute patient loses the ability to carry oxygen, to clot, and to fight infection — all three at once, and quickly. There is no stable holding pattern to wait in. That is the entire reason your week is going the way it is going.

If You Remember One Thing

Memorize the fever rule tonight

When the counts bottom out, a fever is not a symptom you monitor at home. It is the emergency. An infection in someone with no neutrophils can go from “a bit warm” to septic faster than you would believe.

Fever = call now, or go in

The formal criteria doctors use are a single oral temperature of 101°F (38.3°C) or 100.4°F (38.0°C) sustained over an hour.

But most cancer centres tell patients to call at 100.4°F, full stop — because nobody at home should be standing there timing a fever for an hour before picking up the phone. Memorial Sloan Kettering and the American Cancer Society both use the plain 100.4 number for patients.

Ask your team tonight what number they want you to call at, and write it on the whiteboard. Then call at that number, at 3am, on a Sunday, when you feel silly doing it. Nobody will be annoyed with you. That is the entire job.

Things That Confused Us

Four things nobody explained, that we had to figure out

The Hallway

Why they won't let her walk the corridor

Her room was kept at slightly higher air pressure than the hallway, with HEPA-filtered air changed more than twelve times an hour. Clean air flows out; unfiltered hallway air never flows in. Step into the corridor and that protection is gone — and the real hazard is airborne mould spores, especially anywhere near construction dust. That is why patients get masked for transport. It isn't fussiness, it's the CDC standard.

7+3

What the numbers in the chemo name mean

Seven days of cytarabine, running continuously through the IV, plus an anthracycline — usually daunorubicin — on each of the first three days. That's it. That's the name. It's the standard intensive induction for patients well enough to take it, and genetics sometimes add a third drug.

Five Weeks

Why the stay is so much longer than the chemo

The chemo takes seven days. The admission takes four to six weeks. The difference is waiting for the marrow to come back — induction wipes out the healthy cells along with the leukemia, and you cannot leave until your counts recover. Danell's first stay was five weeks, which is squarely typical. Knowing that in advance would have helped us enormously.

Transfusions

The sheer number of them

Nobody warns you about the volume. Published series report a median of roughly 9 to 12 units of red cells and around 7 to 10 platelet transfusions during induction alone — with enormous variation, some patients far more. You will lose count. Somebody donated every one of those, which is worth sitting with for a second.

The One That Blindsided Us

When platelet transfusions stop working

This one deserves its own section, because it happened to Danell, it is genuinely frightening, and the numbers you will find online are mostly out of date and much scarier than the truth.

Partway through treatment, her platelet transfusions stopped holding. Her body had made antibodies against donor platelets, so ordinary units were destroyed almost as fast as they went in. She needed HLA-matched platelets — platelets from a donor whose tissue type matches hers closely enough that her immune system leaves them alone.

Those do not sit on a shelf. They had to find a matching donor, bring that person in, and fly the platelets to us. We spent days in the emergency department with a nosebleed that would not stop, unable to be given the ordinary platelets sitting in the same building, waiting for the right ones to land. That is a specific kind of helpless I had not experienced before.

If this is happening to you right now

The waiting is real and it is documented: blood centres quote 48 to 72 hours to source the first matched unit, longer if your centre has to recruit donors rather than search an existing HLA-typed pool. You are not being deprioritised. Ask your team how big their HLA-typed donor pool is, and whether a larger regional centre can be asked.

And when a good match is found, it usually works — in the largest published series, 84% of matched transfusions produced a solid response.

Now the part nobody told us: the actual odds

If your platelet counts stop responding, the first thing everyone assumes — the thing we assumed — is that the body is rejecting them. That is usually wrong, and the difference matters enormously.

~12–27% of people going through intensive AML treatment will meet a formal definition of platelet refractoriness at some point. Older sources say 30–70%; those are from before modern blood filtering and overstate today's risk.
~2 in 3 of those cases are not immune at all. Fever, infection, bleeding, an enlarged spleen and certain drugs consume platelets just as effectively as antibodies do. In the best study, non-immune factors were the only cause in 67% of failed transfusions.
~5% of patients on this treatment path actually end up needing HLA-matched platelets, down from about 14% before filtered blood products became standard. Danell was in that small group.

That middle number is the one I wish someone had said out loud to us in the ED. “Her body is attacking the platelets” is the least likely explanation, not the most likely. Most of the time the platelets are being eaten by a fever or an infection, which is a very different problem with a very different fix. The only thing that actually settles it is an antibody test — not how fast the counts fell, and not how the transfusion “felt.”

The reason today is better than the older numbers suggest is unglamorous: filtering white cells out of blood products, which nearly every American blood centre now does as standard. When one country switched to it nationally, immune refractoriness in leukaemia patients fell from 14% to 4%. Worth confirming your hospital uses filtered products. Almost all do.

One thing to know if you have been pregnant

Pregnancy exposes you to another person's tissue type, so it raises the chance of carrying the antibodies behind this. In healthy donors, HLA antibodies show up in about 1.7% of women who have never been pregnant, 11% after one pregnancy, and around 32% after four or more.

But carrying antibodies is not the same as becoming refractory — most women with them transfuse perfectly well. And the single biggest risk factor is not pregnancy at all, it is simply how many platelet transfusions you have had. Nobody mentioned any of this to us, and it would have made the whole thing feel less like a mystery.

What to ask, in order

  • “Have you ruled out the non-immune causes?” Fever, infection, bleeding, DIC, an enlarged spleen, graft-versus-host disease — and drugs, especially amphotericin B, vancomycin, heparin and ATG. This is where two thirds of cases actually live.
  • “Has an HLA and HPA antibody screen been sent, and what did it show?” This is the test that decides immune versus non-immune. Everything else is a guess.
  • “Are the post-transfusion counts being drawn 10 to 60 minutes after, not just the next morning?” A next-morning number cannot tell the two apart.
  • “Can we try crossmatch-compatible platelets first?” Often available within 24 hours rather than 48 to 72, and about as effective as a full HLA match.
  • “What match grade are we getting?” Grades A, B1U and B2U work well. C and D matches may perform no better than ordinary platelets.
  • “Should family donate?” Ask this one before anyone volunteers. If a related-donor stem cell transplant is possible, family platelets can create antibodies that interfere with engraftment later. It is easy to miss in the moment and hard to undo.
  • “Is this likely to settle once her counts recover?” For most people it does. That was true for us too, eventually.

One honest note: refractoriness is linked in studies to more bleeding and more transfusions, and researchers are careful about why — it may be a sign of being more seriously ill rather than a cause of worse outcomes. As of 2025 the American Society of Hematology says the effect of matched platelets on survival is still not clear. What is clear is that it makes treatment harder, slower and far more frightening, which is reason enough to be ready for it.

Week One

What actually helped us, immediately

Not the hospital bag list — that's on the caregiver page. These are the first-week ones.

  • Ask for the social worker by name, on day one. Every large cancer centre has one and they are the most underused person in the building. Financial assistance, insurance, FMLA paperwork, lodging near the hospital, transport programmes — all of it runs through them, and almost nobody thinks to ask early.
  • Start a notebook, and write down who said what. You will be handed enormous information by a rotating cast, at the exact moment your brain is least able to hold it. Write the name of the person, the date, and the number. You will be repeating all of it to family within hours.
  • Get a whiteboard for the room. Counts, questions for rounds, laps walked. It gives everyone something to point at, including the patient, and it turns a blur into something you can see.
  • Write your questions down before rounds. The team comes early, moves fast, and every question you didn't write down evaporates the second they walk in.
  • Let people say the thing about a cousin who had cancer. You will hear a lot of it. They mean well and they are frightened too.
  • Accept that you are now the family's press office. Whoever is at the bedside becomes the person translating hematology to twenty relatives on no sleep. Pick one channel — a group text, a private page — and update it once a day rather than answering the same question thirty times.

And say thank you to the floor

The staff on the 11th floor at UK were exceptional. They are good at the medicine, and they are also good at the other thing — the part where two people who had only ever been in a hospital to have a baby suddenly have to live there. If you have landed on a floor like that, you are luckier than you know. Tell them so.
